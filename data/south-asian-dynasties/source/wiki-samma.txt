L189: The Samma dynasty^{【157†[7]】} was a medieval 【140†Sindhi】 【158†Muslim】 dynasty based in 【159†Sindh】.^{【138†[5]】}^{【156†[6]】}^{【160†[8]】} It was established by 【161†Unar bin Babinah】 in 1351 after defeating Hamir (the last 【152†Soomra】 Emir) and declaring independence from the 【122†Delhi Sultanate】.^{【162†[9]】}^{【163†[10]】} The Sammas took the title of 【143†jam】, the equivalent of 【164†sultan】. The capital of the sultanate was in the city of Samanagar (modern-day 【124†Thatta】).
L190: The Sammas were conquered in the early 16th century by the 【153†Arghun dynasty】. The dynasty has left architectural mark in Sindh, most notably at the 【165†Makli Necropolis】.^{【138†[5]】}^{【166†[11]】}
L191: ## Origin
L192: Information about the early years of the Samma dynasty is very sketchy. Tribes such as Samma were regarded as a sub-division of 【167†Jats】 or on a par with the Jats when Muslims first arrived in Sindh.^{【168†[12]】} 【169†Sarah Ansari】 states both 【170†Sammas】 and 【171†Soomros】 to be 【172†Rajput】 tribes who converted to Islam.
L193: Their chiefs were followers of 【173†Suhrawardi】 【174†Sufi】 saints with their base at 【175†Uch】 and 【176†Multan】.^{【177†[13]】} The medieval historian 【178†Firishta】 mentions two groups of 【179†zamindars】 in Sindh, namely Sumra and Samma.^{【180†[14]】}
L194: During most of period of Samma rule, Sindh was politically and economically tied to the 【181†Gujarat Sultanate】, with occasional periods of friction. Coins struck by the Samma dynasty have the titles 【164†sultan】 and 【182†shah】 as well as 【143†jam】.
L195: The 【183†Jadeja】 rulers of western Gujarat were a part of Samma tribe and directly descended from 【144†Jam Unar】, the first Samma sultan of Sindh.^{【184†[15]】} Similarly, the 【185†Chudasama】 Rajputs of Gujarat were too a part of Samma tribe, who remained Hindu, and today inhabit 【186†Junagadh】 and 【187†Bhal region】 of Gujarat.^{【188†[16]】}^{【189†[17]】}
L196: ### Foundation
L197: 
L198:  | This section needs 【69†more citations】. Please help 【70†improve this section】 by 【71†adding citations to reliable sources】. Unsourced material may be challenged and 【72†removed】.
L199: Find sources: 【73†"Samma dynasty"†www.google.com】 – 【74†news†www.google.com】 · 【75†newspapers†www.google.com】 · 【76†books†www.google.com】 · 【77†scholar†scholar.google.com】 · 【78†JSTOR†www.jstor.org】 (September 2026) (【79†Learn how and when to remove this message】)
L200: It is known from 【190†Ibn Battuta】 that in 1333 the Sammas were in rebellion, led by 【145†Jam Tamachi】.^{【191†[18]】} The Sammas overthrew the Soomras soon after 1335 and the last Soomra ruler took shelter with the governor of 【192†Gujarat】, under the protection of 【193†Muhammad bin Tughluq】, the 【194†sultan of Delhi】. Mohammad bin Tughlaq made an expedition against Sindh in 1351 and died at Sondha, possibly in an attempt to restore the Soomras. Following this, the Sammas became independent.
L201: The next sultan, 【195†Firuz Shah Tughlaq】 attacked Sindh in 1365 and 1367, unsuccessfully, but with reinforcements from 【196†Delhi】 he later obtained Banbhiniyo's surrender. For a period the Sammas were therefore subject to Delhi again. Later, as the Sultanate of Delhi collapsed they became completely independent.^{【191†[18]】}
L202: ## History
L203: 
L204: The Samma dynasty took the title "Jam", the equivalent of "King" or "Sultan", because they claimed to be descended from 【197†Jamshid】.^{【198†[19]】} The main sources of information on the Samma dynasty are 【199†Nizamuddin Ahmad】, 【200†Abu 'l-Fazl Allami】, 【178†Firishta】 and 【201†Mir Ma'sum】, all lacking in detail, and with conflicting information. A plausible reconstruction of the chronology is given in the History of Delhi Sultanate by M. H. Syed.^{【198†[19]】}
L205: ### Jam Unar
L206: 
L207: Main article: 【144†Jam Unar】
L208: 
L209: Jam Unar was the founder of Samma dynasty mentioned by 【190†Ibn Battuta】, the famous traveller from 【202†North Africa】, who visited Sindh in 1333, and observed Samma rebellion against the Delhi government.^{【191†[18]】} Jam Unar, the Samma chief, taking advantage of the strained relation between the Soomra and the Sultanate of Delhi, defeated the last Soomra ruler, son of Dodo, and established Samma rule.
L210: ### Jam Salahuddin
L211: 
L212:  | This section does not 【81†cite】 any 【69†sources】. Please help 【70†improve this section】 by 【71†adding citations to reliable sources】. Unsourced material may be challenged and 【72†removed】. (September 2026) (【79†Learn how and when to remove this message】)
L213: Jám Saláhuddìn bin Jám Tamáchí was the successor of his father Jám Tamáchí. He put down revolts in some parts of the country, by sending forces in those directions and punished the ringleaders. Some of these unruly bands fled to Kachh, to which place Jám Saláhuddín pursued them, and in every engagement that took place he defeated them and ultimately subjugated them. He died after a reign of 11 years.
L214: ### Jam Ali Sher
L215: 
L216: Main article: 【203†Jam Ali Sher】
L217: 
L218:  | This section does not 【81†cite】 any 【69†sources】. Please help 【70†improve this section】 by 【71†adding citations to reliable sources】. Unsourced material may be challenged and 【72†removed】. (September 2026) (【79†Learn how and when to remove this message】)
L219: Jám Alí Sher bin Jám Tamáchí ruled the country very discreetly. Tamáchí's other sons Sikandar and Karn, and Fateh Khán son of Sikandar, who had brought ruin on the last Jám, were now conspiring against Jám Alísher. They were therefore looking for an opportunity to fall upon him while he was out enjoying the moonlight as usual. They spent their time in the forests in the vicinity of the town.
L220: One Friday night, on the 13th of the lunar month, they took a band of cut-throats with them, and with naked swords attacked Jám Alísher who had come out in a boat to enjoy the moonlight on the quiet surface of the river and was returning home. They killed him, and red-handed they ran to the city, where the people had no help for it but to place one of them, Karan, on the vacant throne. The reign of Jám Alí Sher lasted for seven years.
L221: ### Jam Fateh Khan bin Jam Sikandar
L222: 
L223:  | This section does not 【81†cite】 any 【69†sources】. Please help 【70†improve this section】 by 【71†adding citations to reliable sources】. Unsourced material may be challenged and 【72†removed】. (September 2026) (【79†Learn how and when to remove this message】)
L224: 
L225: 【204†Jam Karan】 was succeeded by his nephew Jám Fateh Khán bin Sikandar. He ruled quietly for some time and gave satisfaction to the people in general.
L226: About this time, 【205†Mirza】 【206†Pir Muhammad】 one of 【207†Amir Timur】’s grandsons came to 【176†Multan】 and conquered that town and 【175†Uch】. As he made a long stay there, most of the horses with him died of a disease and his horsemen were obliged to move about as foot-soldiers. When Amir Timur heard of this, he sent 30,000 horses from his own stables to his grandson to enable him to extend his conquests.
L227: Pir Muhammad, being thus equipped, attacked those of the 【208†zamindars】 who had threatened to do him harm and destroyed their household property. He then sent a messenger to Bakhar calling the chief men of the place to come and pay respects to him. But these men fearing his vengeance left the place in a body and went to Jesalmer. Only one solitary person, Sayyed Abulghais, one of the pious Sayyeds of the place, went to visit the Mirzá.
L228: He interceded for his town-people in the name of his great grandfather, the Prophet, and the Mirzá accepted his intercession.
L229: Mirzá Pír Muhammad soon went to Delhi, which place he took and where he was crowned as king. 【176†Multan】 remained in the hands of Langáhs, and Sind in those of the Sammah rulers as before.
L230: ### Jam Tughlaq
L231: 
L232:  | This section does not 【81†cite】 any 【69†sources】. Please help 【70†improve this section】 by 【71†adding citations to reliable sources】. Unsourced material may be challenged and 【72†removed】. (September 2026) (【79†Learn how and when to remove this message】)
L233: Tomb 【209†Jam Mubarak Khan】, a Samma Prince, at the 【165†Makli Necropolis】
L234: Jám Taghlak was fond of hunting and left his brothers to administer the affairs of state at Sehwán and Bakhar. In his reign some Balóch raised the standard of revolt in the outskirts of Bakhar, but Jám Taghlak marched in the direction and punished their ring-leaders and appointed an outpost in each 【210†parganah】 to prevent any future rebellion of the kind. He died after a reign of 28 years.
L235: ### Jam Sikandar II
L236: 
L237: Main article: 【211†Jam Sikandar II】
L238: 
L239:  | This section does not 【81†cite】 any 【69†sources】. Please help 【70†improve this section】 by 【71†adding citations to reliable sources】. Unsourced material may be challenged and 【72†removed】. (September 2026) (【79†Learn how and when to remove this message】)
L240: Jám Sikandar bin Jám Fateh was a minor when he succeeded his father to the throne. The governors of Sehwán and Bakhar shook off their yoke, and prepared to take offensive steps. Jám Sikandar was obliged to march out from Tattá to Bakhar. When he came as far as Nasarpúr, a man by name Mubárak, who during the last Jám's reign had made himself celebrated for acts of bravery, proclaimed himself king under the name of Jám Mubárak.
L241: But as the people were not in league with him, he was driven away within 3 days and information sent to Jám Sikandar, who made peace with his opponents and hastened to Tattá. After a year and a half, he died.
L242: ### Jam Nizamuddin I
L243: 
L244: Main article: 【212†Jam Nizamuddin I】
L245: 
L246:  | This section does not 【81†cite】 any 【69†sources】. Please help 【70†improve this section】 by 【71†adding citations to reliable sources】. Unsourced material may be challenged and 【72†removed】. (September 2026) (【79†Learn how and when to remove this message】)
L247: After Jam Salahuddin's death, the nobles of the state put his son Jám Nizámuddín I bin Jám Saláhuddín on the throne. Jam Nizamuddin ruled for only a few months. His first act of kindness was the release of his cousins Sikandar, Karn and Baháuddín and Ámar, who had been placed in captivity by the advice of the ministers.
L248: He appointed every one of them as an officer to discharge administrative duties in different places, while he himself remained in the capital, superintending the work done by them and other officials in different quarters of the country.
L249: Before long, however, his cousins, very ungratefully made a conspiracy among themselves and stealthily coming to the capital attempted to seize him. But Jám Saláhuddín learning their intention in time, left the place at the dead of night with a handful of men and made his escape to 【213†Gujrat】. In the morning, men were sent after him, but before any information could be brought about him, the people summoned Alísher, son of Jám Tamáchí, who was living in obscurity, and raised him to the throne.
L250: Meanwhile, Jám Nizámuddín also died in his flight and his cousins too being disappointed in every thing, lived roving lives.
L251: ### Jam Sanjar
L252: 
L253: Main article: 【146†Jam Sanjar】
L254: 
L255:  | This section does not 【81†cite】 any 【69†sources】. Please help 【70†improve this section】 by 【71†adding citations to reliable sources】. Unsourced material may be challenged and 【72†removed】. (September 2026) (【79†Learn how and when to remove this message】)
L256: On Ráinah's death, Sanjar (Radhan) Sadr al-Din became the Jám of Sind. He is said to have been a very handsome person, and on that account was constantly attended by a large number of persons, who took pleasure in remaining in his company.
L257: It is believed that before his coming to the throne, a pious fakír had been very fond of him; that one day Sanjar informed him that he had a very strong desire to become the king of Tattá though it should be for not more than 8 days; and that the fakír had given him his blessings, telling him that he would be the king of the place for 8 years.
L258: Jám Sanjar ruled the country very wisely. Under no ruler before this had the people of Sind enjoyed such ease of mind. He was very fond of the company of the learned and the pious. Every Friday he used to distribute charities and had fixed periodical allowances for those who deserved the same. He increased the pay of responsible officers.
L259: One Kází Maarúf, who had been appointed by the late rulers to be the Kází of Bakhar, was in the habit of receiving bribes from the plaintiffs as well as from the defendants. When this fact came to the notice of Jám Sanjar, he sent for the Kází and asked him about it. The Kází admitted the whole thing.
L260: "Yes", said he, "I do demand something from the plaintiffs as well as the defendants, and I am anxious to get something from the witnesses too, but before the case closes, they go away and I am disappointed in that". Jám Sanjar could not help laughing at this. The Kází continued: "I work in the court for the whole day and my wife and children die of hunger at home, because I get very little pay".
L261: Jám Sanjar increased his pay and issued general orders for the increase of every government post of importance.

L268: Jám Nizámuddín II (866–914 AH, 1461–1508 AD) was the most famous Sultan of the Samma or Jamot dynasty,^{【216†[21]】} which ruled in Sindh and parts of 【217†Punjab】 and 【218†Balochistan】 (region) from 1351–1551 CE. He was known by the nickname of Jám Nindó. His capital was at Thatta in modern Pakistan. The Samma Sultanate reached the height of its power during the reign of Jam Nizamuddin II, who is still recalled as a hero, and his rule as a golden age.
L269: Shortly after his accession, he went with a large force to 【219†Bhakkar】, where he spent about a year, during which time he extirpated the freebooters and robbers who annoyed the people in that part of the country. After that, for a period of forty-eight years he reigned at Tatta with absolute power.
L270: In the last part of Jám Nindó's reign, after 1490 CE, a Mughul army under Shah Beg Arghun came from 【220†Kandahar】 and fell upon many villages of Chundooha and Sideejuh, invading the town of Ágrí, Ohándukah, Sibi Sindichah and Kót Máchián. Jám Nindó sent a large army under his Vazier Darya Khan,^{【221†[22]】} which, arriving at the village known by the name of Duruh-i-Kureeb, also known as Joolow Geer or Halúkhar near Sibi, defeated the Mughuls in a pitched battle.
L271: Sháh Beg Arghun's brother Abú Muhammad Mirzá was killed in the battle, and the Mughuls fled back to Kandahár, never to return during the reign of Jám Nizámuddín.^{【222†[23]】}
L272: Jam Nizamuddin's death was followed by a war of succession between the cousins Jam Feroz and Jam Salahuddin.

L279: Jam Feruz bin Jam Nizam was the last ruler of the Samma dynasty of Sindh. Jám Feróz succeeded his father Jám Nizámuddín at a minor age. Jám Feróz was a young man, and as from the commencement the management of the state affairs was in the hands of his guardian he spent his time in his harem and seldom went out. But he was fearful of his ministers.
L280: As a precautionary measure he enlisted in his service Kíbak Arghún and a large number of men belonging to the tribes of Mughuls, who had during his reign, left Sháhbeg Arghún and came to Tattá. Jám Feróz gave them the quarter of the town, called Mughal-Wárah to live in. He secretly flattered himself for his policy in securing the services of intrepid men to check Daryá Khán, but he never for a minute imagined what ruin these very men were destined to bring on him.
L281: For, it was through some of these men that Sháhbeg Arghún was induced to invade and conquer Sind in 926 AH (1519 AD) at the 【224†Battle of Fatehpur】, which resulted in the displacement of the Sammah dynasty of rulers by that of Arghún.
L282: ## Legacy
L283: 
L284: 【165†Makli Necropolis】 features several clusters of elaborate funerary monuments dating between the 14th and 18th centuries. The site rose to prominence as a major funerary site during under the rule of the Samma dynasty, who had made their capital near 【124†Thatta】.^{【225†[24]】}
L285: The rise of Thatta as an important commercial and cultural centre was directly related to Jam Ninda's patronage and policies. At the time the 【226†Portuguese took control】 of the trading centre of 【227†Hormuz】 in 1514 CE,^{【228†[25]】} trade from the Sindh accounted for nearly 10% of their customs revenue, and they described Thatta as one of the richest cities in the world.
L286: Thatta's prosperity was based partly on its own high-quality cotton and silk textile industry, partly on export of goods from further inland in the 【217†Punjab】 and 【229†northern India】. However, the trade declined when the Mughals took over. Later, due to silting of the main Indus channel, Thatta no longer functioned as a port.^{【230†[26]】}
L287: The Samma civilization contributed significantly to the evolution of the 【231†Indo-Islamic architectural】 style. Thatta is famous for its necropolis, which covers 10 square km on the 【232†Makli Hill】. It assumed its quasi-sacred character during Jam Ninda's rule. Every year thousands perform pilgrimage to this site to commemorate the saints buried here. The graves testify to a long period when Thatta was a thriving center of trade, religion and scholarly pursuits.^{【233†[27]】}
