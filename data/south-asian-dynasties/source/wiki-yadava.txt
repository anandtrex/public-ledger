L170: The Seuna, Sevuna, or 【111†Yadavas】 of Devagiri (【112†IAST】: Seuṇa, c. (circa) 1187–1317)^{【113†[5]】} was a 【114†medieval Indian】 dynasty, which at its peak ruled a realm stretching from the 【115†Narmada】 river in the north to the 【116†Tungabhadra】 river in the south, in the western part of the 【117†Deccan】 region.
L171: Its territory included present-day 【118†Maharashtra】, 【119†northern Karnataka】 and parts of 【120†Madhya Pradesh】, from its capital at 【102†Devagiri】 (present-day 【121†Daulatabad】 in modern 【122†Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar district】, Maharashtra).
L172: The Yadavas initially ruled as feudatories of the 【123†Western Chalukyas】. Around the middle of the 12th century, as the Chalukya power waned, the Yadava king 【75†Bhillama V】 declared independence. The Yadavas reached their peak under 【124†Simhana II】, and flourished until the early 14th century, when it was annexed by the 【125†Khalji dynasty】 of the 【126†Delhi Sultanate】 in 1308 CE.
L173: ## Etymology
L174: 
L175: The Seuna dynasty claimed descent from the 【111†Yadavas】 and therefore, its kings are often referred to as the "Yadavas of Devagiri". The correct name of the 【127†dynasty】, however, is Seuna or Sevuna.^{【128†[6]】} The inscriptions of this dynasty, as well as those of contemporary kingdoms and empires, the 【129†Hoysalas】, the 【88†Kakatiyas】 and the 【123†Western Chalukyas】 call them Seunas.^{【130†[7]】} The name is probably derived from the name of their second ruler, "Seunachandra".
L176: The "Sevuna" (or Seuna) name was brought back into use by 【131†John Faithfull Fleet】 in his 1894 book The dynasties of the 【132†Kanarese】 districts of the Bombay Presidency from the earliest historical times to the Musalman conquest of A.D. 1318.^{【133†[8]】}^{【134†[9]】}
L177: ## Origin
L178: 
L179: 【135†Gondeshwar Temple】 was built during the Seuna (Yadava) reign
L180: 
L181: The earliest historical ruler of the Seuna/Yadava dynasty can be dated to the mid-9th century, but the origin of the dynasty is uncertain.^{【113†[5]】} Little is known about their early history: their 13th century court poet 【136†Hemadri】 records the names of the family's early rulers, but his information about the pre-12th century rulers is often incomplete and inaccurate.^{【137†[10]】}
L182: The dynasty claimed descent from 【138†Yadu】, a legendary hero mentioned in the 【139†Puranas】.^{【137†[10]】} According to this account, found in Hemadri's Vratakhanda as well as several inscriptions,^{【113†[5]】} their ancestors originally resided at 【140†Mathura】, and then migrated to 【141†Dvaraka】 (Dvaravati) in present-day 【142†Gujarat】.
L183: A 【143†Jain】 legend states that the Jain saint Jinaprabhasuri saved the pregnant mother of the dynasty's founder 【144†Dridhaprahara】 from a great fire that destroyed Dvaraka. A family feudatory to the Yadavas migrated from 【145†Vallabhi】 (also in present-day Gujarat) to 【146†Khandesh】. But otherwise, no historical evidence corroborates their connection to Dvaraka.
L184: The dynasty never tried to conquer Dvaraka, or establish any political or cultural connections with that region.^{【137†[10]】} Its rulers started claiming to be descendants of Yadu and migrants from Dvaraka after becoming politically prominent.^{【147†[11]】} Dvaraka was associated with 【111†Yadu's descendants】, and the dynasty's claim of connection with that city may simply be a result of their claim of descent from Yadu rather than their actual geographic origin.^{【148†[12]】} The 【149†Hoysalas】, the southern neighbours of the dynasty, similarly claimed descent from Yadu and claimed to be the former lords of Dvaraka.^{【147†[11]】} But there are no early records directly linking the Seuna Yadavas and Hoysala Yadavas to the Yadavas of 【150†North India】.^{【151†[13]】}^{【152†[14]】}
L185: Hero stone (Virgal) with 【153†Old Kannada】 inscription dated 1286 CE from the rule of Yadava King Ramachandra in Kedareshvara temple at Balligavi in Shimoga district, Karnataka state
L186: The territory of the early Yadava rulers was located in present-day 【118†Maharashtra】,^{【147†[11]】} and several scholars (especially Maharashtrian historians^{【154†[15]】}) have claimed a "【155†Maratha】" origin for the dynasty,^{【156†[16]】} such as H.V Trivedi and Y.A.
L187: Raikar who claim 【105†Marathi】 was their native language.^{【157†[17]】}^{【158†[18]】} However, Marathi the language of present-day Maharashtra, began to appear as the dominant language in the dynasty's inscriptions only in the 14th century, before which 【159†Kannada】 and 【104†Sanskrit】 were the primary language of their inscriptions.^{【160†[19]】}^{【154†[15]】} Marathi appears in around two hundred Yadava inscriptions, but usually as translation of or addition to Kannada and Sanskrit text.
L188: During the last half century of the dynasty's rule, it became the dominant language of epigraphy, which may have been a result of the Yadava attempts to connect with their Marathi-speaking subjects, and to distinguish themselves from the Kannada-speaking Hoysalas.^{【154†[15]】} The earliest instance of the Yadavas using the term "marathe" as a self-designation appears in a 1311 inscription recording a donation to the 【161†Pandharpur temple】,^{【162†[20]】} towards the end of the dynasty's rule.^{【156†[16]】} However the word was not used to indicate the Maratha caste but meant “belonging to Maharashtra”.^{【163†[21]】}
L189: Epigraphic evidence suggests that the dynasty likely emerged from a Kannada-speaking background.^{【164†[22]】} Around five hundred Yadava inscriptions have been discovered, and Kannada is the most common language of these inscriptions, followed by Sanskrit.^{【154†[15]】} Of the inscriptions found in present-day 【165†Karnataka】 (the oldest being from the reign of Bhillama II), most are in Kannada language and script; others are in the Kannada language but use 【166†Devanagari】 script.^{【130†[7]】} Older inscriptions from Karnataka also attest to the existence of Yadava feudatories (such as Seunas of Masavadi) ruling in the 【167†Dharwad】 region in the 9th century, although these feudatories cannot be connected to the main line of the dynasty with certainty.^{【113†[5]】}^{【147†[11]】} Many of the dynasty's rulers had Kannada names and titles such as "Dhadiyappa", "Bhillama", "Rajugi", "Vadugi" and "Vasugi", and "Kaliya Ballala".
L190: Some kings had names like "Simhana" (or "Singhana") and "Mallugi", which were also used by the 【168†Kalachuris of Kalyani】, who ruled in present-day Karnataka. Records show that one of the early rulers, Seunachandra II, had a Kannada title, Sellavidega. The rulers had very close matrimonial relationships with Kannada-speaking royal families throughout their rule. Bhillama II was married to Lachchiyavve, who was from a 【169†Rashtrakuta】 descendant family in Karnataka.
L191: Vaddiga was married to Vaddiyavve, daughter of Rashtrakuta chieftain Dhorappa. Wives of Vesugi and Bhillama III were 【170†Chalukya】 princesess. The early Seuna coins also had Kannada legends engraved on them indicating it was a court language.^{【130†[7]】} The early Yadavas may have migrated northwards owing to the political situation in the 【171†Deccan】 region,^{【172†[23]】} or may have been dispatched by their Rashtrakuta overlords to rule the northern regions.^{【134†[9]】}
L192: ## Political history
L193: ### As feudatories
L194: 
L195: The fortress of 【102†Devagiri】, the capital of Yadavas Hero stone with old Kannada inscription dated 1235. from the rule of Yadava King 【124†Simhana II】 at Kubetur, Soraba Taluk, Shimoga district, Karnataka state
L196: The earliest historically attested ruler of the dynasty is 【144†Dridhaprahara】 (c. 860–880), who is said to have established the city of Chandradityapura (modern 【173†Chandor】).^{【147†[11]】}^{【113†[5]】} He probably rose to prominence by protecting the people of 【146†Khandesh】 region from enemy raiders, amid the instability brought by the 【174†Pratihara-Rashtrakuta war】.^{【147†[11]】}
L197: Dridhaprahara's son and successor was Seunachandra (c. 880–900), after whom the dynasty was called Seuna-vamsha (【112†IAST】: Seuṇa-vaṃśa) and their territory was called Seuna-desha.^{【175†[24]】}^{【147†[11]】} He probably became a 【169†Rashtrakuta】 feudatory after helping the Rashtrakutas against their northern neighbours, the 【86†Paramaras】.^{【175†[24]】} He established a new town called Seunapura (possibly modern 【176†Sinnar】).^{【147†[11]】}
L198: Not much information is available about Seunachandra's successors — Dhadiyappa (or Dadhiyappa), Bhillama I, and Rajugi (or Rajiga) — who ruled during c. 900–950.^{【175†[24]】}^{【177†[25]】} The next ruler Vandugi (also Vaddiga I or Baddiga) raised the family's political status by marrying into the imperial Rashtrakuta family. He married Vohivayya, a daughter of Dhorappa, who was a younger brother of the Rashtrakuta emperor 【178†Krishna III】.
L199: Vandugi participated in Krishna's military campaigns, which may have resulted in an increase in his fief, although this cannot be said with certainty.^{【177†[25]】}
L200: Little is known about the next ruler, Dhadiyasa (c. 970–985).^{【177†[25]】} His son 【179†Bhillama II】 acknowledged the suzerainty of the 【180†Kalyani Chalukya】 ruler 【181†Tailapa II】, who overthrew the Rashtrakutas. As a Chalukya feudatory, he played an important role in Tailapa's victory over the 【86†Paramara】 king 【182†Munja】.^{【175†[24]】} Bhillama II was succeeded by Vesugi I (r. c. 1005–1025), who married Nayilladevi, the daughter of a Chalukya feudatory of Gujarat.
L201: The next ruler Bhillama III is known from his Kalas Budruk grant inscription.^{【183†[26]】} He married Avalladevi, a daughter of the Chalukya king 【184†Jayasimha II】, as attested by a 【185†Vasai】 (Bassein) inscription. He may have helped his father-in-law Jayasimha and his brother-in-law 【186†Someshvara I】 in their campaigns against the Paramara king 【187†Bhoja】.^{【175†[24]】}^{【183†[26]】}
L202: For unknown reasons, the Yadava power seems to have declined over the next decade, during the reigns of Vesugi II (alias Vaddiga or Yadugi) and Bhillama IV. The next ruler was Seunachandra II, who, according to the Yadava records, restored the family's fortunes just like the god 【188†Hari】 had restored the earth's fortunes with his 【189†varaha】 incarnation. Seunachandra II appears to have ascended the throne around 1050, as he is attested by the 1052 【190†Deolali】 inscription.
L203: He bore the feudatory title Maha-mandaleshvara and became the overlord of several sub-feudatories, including a family of Khandesh. A 1069 inscription indicates that he had a ministry of seven officers, all of whom bore high-sounding titles.^{【183†[26]】} During his tenure, the Chalukya kingdom saw a war of succession between the brothers 【191†Someshvara II】 and 【192†Vikramaditya VI】.
L204: Seunachandra II supported Vikramaditya (who ultimately succeeded), and rose to the position of Maha-mandaleshvara.^{【175†[24]】} His son Airammadeva (or Erammadeva, r. c. 1085–1105), who helped him against Someshvara II, succeeded him. Airammadeva's queen was Yogalla, but little else is known about his reign.^{【193†[27]】} The Asvi inscription credits him with helping place Vikramaditya on the Chalukya throne.^{【183†[26]】}
L205: Airammadeva was succeeded by his brother Simhana I (r. c. 1105–1120).^{【194†[28]】} The Yadava records state that he helped his overlord Vikramaditya VI complete the Karpura-【195†vrata】 ritual, by getting him a karpura elephant. An 1124 inscription mentions that he was ruling the Paliyanda-4000 province (identified as the area around modern 【196†Paranda】).^{【193†[27]】} The dynasty's history over the next fifty years is obscure.
L206: The 1142 【197†Anjaneri】 inscription attests the rule of a person named Seunachandra, but Hemadri's records of the dynasty do not mention any Seunachandra III; historian 【198†R. G. Bhandarkar】 theorized that this Seunachandra may have been a Yadava sub-feudatory.^{【199†[29]】}
L207: The next known ruler Mallugi (r. c. 1145–1160) was a loyal feudatory to the Chalukya king 【200†Tailapa III】. His general Dada and Dada's son Mahidhara fought with Tailapa's rebellious 【168†Kalachuri】 feudatory 【201†Bijjala II】.
L208: He extended his territory by capturing Parnakheta (modern Patkhed in 【202†Akola district】).^{【199†[29]】} The Yadava records claim that he seized the elephants of the king of 【203†Utkala】, but do not provide any details.^{【193†[27]】} He also raided the kingdom of the 【204†Kakatiya】 ruler Rudra, but this campaign did not result in any territorial gains for him.^{【199†[29]】} Mallugi was succeeded by his elder son Amara-gangeya, who was succeeded by his son Amara-mallugi (alias Mallugi II).
L209: The next ruler Kaliya-ballala, whose relationship to Mallugi is unknown, was probably an usurper. He was succeeded by 【75†Bhillama V】 around 1175.^{【199†[29]】}
L210: ### Rise as a sovereign power
L211: At the time of 【75†Bhillama V】's ascension in c. 1175, his nominal overlords — the 【180†Chalukyas】 — were busy fighting their former feudatories, such as the 【149†Hoysalas】 and the 【168†Kalachuris】.^{【205†[30]】} Bhillama raided the northern 【206†Gujarat Chaulukya】 and 【86†Paramara】 territories, although these invasions did not result in any territorial annexations.
L212: The 【207†Naddula Chahamana】 ruler 【208†Kelhana】, who was a Gujarat Chaulukya feudatory, forced him to retreat.^{【209†[31]】} Meanwhile, the Hoysala ruler 【210†Ballala II】 invaded the Chalukya capital Kalyani, forcing Bhillama's overlord 【211†Someshvara】 to flee.^{【74†[1]】}
L213: Around 1187, Bhillama forced Ballala to retreat, conquered the former Chalukya capital Kalyani, and declared himself a sovereign ruler.^{【74†[1]】} He then established 【102†Devagiri】, a formidable natural stronghold, which became the new Yadava capital.^{【212†[32]】}^{【213†[33]】}
L214: In the late 1180s, Ballala launched a campaign against Bhillama, and decisively defeated his army at 【214†Soratur】.^{【215†[34]】} The Yadavas were driven to the north of the 【216†Malaprabha】 and 【217†Krishna】 rivers, which formed the Yadava-Hoysala border for the next two decades.^{【215†[34]】}

L262: The Yadavas and main contemporary Asian polities circa 1205.^{【243†[35]】}
L263: 
L264: Bhillama's son 【244†Jaitugi】 successfully invaded the 【204†Kakatiya】 kingdom around 1194, and forced them to accept the Yadava suzerainty.^{【245†[36]】}^{【246†[37]】}
L265: Jaitugi's son 【124†Simhana】, who succeeded him around either 1200^{【247†[38]】} or 1210,^{【248†[39]】} is regarded as the dynasty's greatest ruler.^{【247†[38]】} At its height, his kingdom probably extended from the 【115†Narmada River】 in the north to the 【249†Tungabhadra River】 in the south, and from the 【250†Arabian Sea】 in the west to the western part of the present-day 【251†Telangana】 in the east.^{【252†[40]】} He launched a military campaign against the Hoysalas (who were engaged in a war with the 【253†Pandyas】), and captured a substantial part of their territory.^{【254†[41]】}^{【255†[42]】} The 【256†Rattas of Saundatti】, who formerly acknowledged the Hoysala suzerainty, became his feudatories, and helped him expand the Yadava power southwards.^{【255†[42]】} In 1215, Simhana successfully invaded the northern 【86†Paramara】 kingdom.
L266: According to Hemadri, this invasion resulted in the death of the Paramara king 【257†Arjunavarman】, although this claim is of doubtful veracity.^{【258†[43]】} Around 1216, Simhana defeated the 【259†Kohalpur Shilahara】 king 【260†Bhoja II】, a former feudatory, who had asserted his sovereignty. The Shilahara kingdom, including its capital 【261†Kolhapur】, was annexed to the Yadava kingdom as a result of this victory.^{【262†[44]】}^{【255†[42]】}
L267: In 1220, Simhana sent an army to the 【263†Lata】 region in present-day 【142†Gujarat】, whose rulers kept shifting his allegiance between the Yadavas, the Paramaras, and the 【206†Chaulukyas】.^{【264†[45]】} Simhana's general Kholeshvara killed the defending ruler Simha, and captured Lata.^{【265†[46]】} Simhana then appointed Simha's son Shankha as a Yadava vassal in Lata.^{【266†[47]】} Sometime later, the Chaulukya general Lavanaprasada invaded Lata, and captured the important port city of 【267†Khambhat】.
L268: Simhana's feudatory Shankha invaded Chaulukya-controlled territory twice, with his help, but was forced to retreat.^{【268†[48]】} The Chaulukya-Yadava conflict came to end in c. 1232 with a peace treaty.^{【269†[49]】} In the 1240s, Lavanaprasada's grandson Visaladeva usurped the power in Gujarat, and became the first 【85†Vagehla】 monarch. During his reign, Simhana's forces invaded Gujarat unsuccessfully, and the Yadava general Rama (a son of Kholeshvara) was killed in a battle.^{【270†[50]】}
L269: The 【271†Aundha Nagnath Temple】 was built by the Yadavas in the 13th century CE.
L270: Several Yadava feudatories kept shifting their allegiance between the Yadavas and the Hoysalas, and tried to assert their independence whenever presented with an opportunity. Simhana's general Bichana subdued several such chiefs, including the Rattas, the Guttas of 【167†Dharwad】, the 【272†Kadambas of Hangal】, and the 【273†Kadambas of Goa】.^{【274†[51]】} The Kakatiya king Ganapati served him as a feudatory for several years, but assumed independence towards the end of his reign.
L271: However, Ganapati did not adopt an aggressive attitude towards the Yadavas, so no major conflict happened between the two dynasties during Simhana's reign.^{【275†[52]】}
L272: Simhana was succeeded by his grandson 【276†Krishna】 (alias Kannara), who invaded the Paramara kingdom, which had weakened because of invasions from the 【126†Delhi Sultanate】.
L273: He defeated the Paramara king sometime before 1250, although this victory did not result in any territorial annexation.^{【277†[53]】} Krishna also attempted an invasion of the Vaghela-ruled Gujarat, but this conflict was inconclusive, with both sides claiming victory.^{【277†[53]】}^{【278†[54]】} He also fought against the Hoysalas; again, both sides claim victory in this conflict.^{【278†[54]】}
L274: Krishna's younger brother and successor 【279†Mahadeva】 curbed a rebellion by the 【280†Shilaharas】 of northern 【281†Konkan】, whose ruler 【282†Someshvara】 had attempted to assert his sovereignty.^{【283†[55]】} He invaded the eastern 【204†Kakatiya】 kingdom, taking advantage of rebellions against the Kakatiya queen 【284†Rudrama】,^{【285†[56]】} but this invasion appears to have been repulsed.^{【286†[57]】} He also invaded the southern 【149†Hoysala】 kingdom, but this invasion was repulsed by the Hoysala king 【287†Narasimha II】.^{【285†[56]】} Mahadeva's Kadamba feudatories rebelled against him, but this rebellion was suppressed by his general Balige-deva around 1268.^{【285†[56]】}
L275: Mahadeva was succeeded by his son 【288†Ammana】, who was dethroned by Krishna's son 【289†Ramachandra】 after a short reign in 1270.^{【290†[58]】}^{【291†[59]】} During the first half of his reign, Ramachandra adopted an aggressive policy against his neighbours.
