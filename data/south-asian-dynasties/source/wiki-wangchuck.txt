L150: The Wangchuck dynasty (【76†Tibetan】: དབང་ཕྱུག་རྒྱལ་རྒྱུད་, 【77†Wylie】: Dbang-phyug Rgyal-rgyud) is the ruling family of 【67†Bhutan】, having held the hereditary position of 【78†Druk Gyalpo】 ("Dragon King") since 1907. Prior to reunification, the Wangchuck family had governed the district of 【79†Trongsa】 as descendants of Dungkar Choji. They eventually overpowered other regional lords and earned the favour of the 【80†British Empire】.
L151: After consolidating power, the 12th 【81†Penlop of Trongsa】 Gongsar 【68†Ugyen Wangchuck】 was elected Druk Gyalpo, thus founding the dynasty. The position of Druk Gyalpo – who heads the royal family of Bhutan – is more commonly known in English as the King of Bhutan, however "Druk Gyalpo" would be translated literally as "Dragon King" (or less commonly, "King of the Dragons", or "Thunder Dragon King")
L152: The Wangchuck dynasty ruled government power in Bhutan and established relations with the 【80†British Empire】 and 【82†India】 under its first two monarchs. The third, fourth, and fifth (current) monarchs have put the kingdom on its path toward 【83†democratization】, 【84†decentralization】, and development.
L153: ## History
L154: 
L155: [【85†edit】]
L156: 
L157: Genealogy of the Wangchuck Dynasty of Bhutan
L158: 
L159: There have been five Wangchuck kings of Bhutan, namely:
L160:   1. 【68†Ugyen Wangchuck】 (b.1861–d.1926) "First King"; reigned 17 December 1907 – 21 August 1926.
L161:   2. 【86†Jigme Wangchuck】 (b.1905–d.1952) "Second King"; r. 21 August 1926 – 24 March 1952.
L162:   3. 【87†Jigme Dorji Wangchuck】 (b.1929–d.1972) "Third King"; r. 24 March 1952 – 24 July 1972.
L163:   4. 【88†Jigme Singye Wangchuck】 (b.1955) "Fourth King"; r. 24 July 1972 – 9 December 2006.
L164:   5. 【69†Jigme Khesar Namgyel Wangchuck】 (b.1980) "Fifth King"; r. 9 December 2006 – present.
L165: The ascendency of the Wangchuck family is deeply rooted in the historical politics of 【67†Bhutan】. Between 1616 and 1907, varying administrative, religious, and regional powers vied for control within Bhutan. During this period, factions were influenced and supported by 【89†Tibet】 and the 【80†British Empire】. Ultimately, the hereditary 【81†Penlop of Trongsa】, 【68†Ugyen Wangchuck】, was elected the first 【78†Druk Gyalpo】 by an assembly of his subjects in 1907, marking the ascendency of his dynasty.
L166: ### Origins
L167: 
L168: [【90†edit】]
L169: 
L170: Further information: 【91†History of Bhutan】 and 【92†Tibetan dual system of government】
L171: Under Bhutan's early theocratic 【92†Tibetan dual system of government】, decreasingly effective central government control resulted in the 【93†de facto】 disintegration of the office of 【94†Shabdrung】 after the death of Shabdrung 【95†Ngawang Namgyal】 in 1651. Under the dual system of the government, Desi or the temporal rulers took control of civil administration and Je Khenpos took control of religious affairs.
L172: Two successor Shabdrungs – the son (1651) and stepbrother (1680) of Ngawang Namgyal – were effectively controlled by the 【96†Druk Desi】 and 【97†Je Khenpo】 until power was further splintered through the innovation of multiple Shabdrung incarnations, reflecting speech, mind, and body.
L173: Increasingly secular regional lords (【98†penlops】 and 【99†dzongpons】) competed for power amid a backdrop of civil war over the Shabdrung and invasions from 【89†Tibet】, and the 【100†Mongol Empire】.^{【101†[1]】} The penlops of 【72†Trongsa】 and 【102†Paro】, and the 【103†dzongpons】 of 【104†Punakha】, 【105†Thimphu】, and 【106†Wangdue Phodrang】 were particularly notable figures in the competition for regional dominance.^{【101†[1]】}^{【107†[2]】}
L174: 【108†Chogyal】 Minjur Tenpa (1613–1680; r. 1667–1680) was the first 【81†Penlop of Trongsa】 (Tongsab), appointed by Shabdrung 【95†Ngawang Namgyal】. He was born Damchho Lhundrub in Min-Chhud, 【89†Tibet】, and led a monastic life from childhood. Before his appointment as Tongsab, he held the appointed post of Umzey (Chant Master). A trusted follower of the Shabdrung, Minjur Tenpa was sent to subdue kings of Bumthang, Lhuntse, Trashigang, Zhemgang, and other lords from 【109†Trongsa Dzong】.
L175: After doing so, the Tongsab divided his control in the east among eight regions (Shachho Khorlo Tsegay), overseen by Dungpas and Kutshabs (civil servants). He went on to build 【110†Jakar】, 【111†Lhuntse】, Trashigang, and Zhemgang Dzongs.^{【112†[3]】}^{: 106 }
L176: Within this political landscape, the Wangchuck family originated in the 【73†Bumthang region】 of central Bhutan.^{【113†[4]】} The family belongs to the Nyö clan, and is descended from 【114†Pema Lingpa】, a Bhutanese 【115†Nyingmapa】 saint. The Nyö clan emerged as a local aristocracy, supplanting many older aristocratic families of Tibetan origin that sided with Tibet during invasions of Bhutan.
L177: In doing so, the clan came to occupy the hereditary position of 【81†Penlop of Trongsa】, as well as significant national and local government positions.^{【116†[5]】}
L178: The 【81†Penlop of Trongsa】 managed central Bhutan; the rival Penlop of Paro controlled western Bhutan; and dzongpons controlled areas surrounding their respective 【117†dzongs】. The Penlop of Paro, unlike Trongsa, was an office appointed by the 【96†Druk Desi】's central government. Because western regions controlled by the Penlop of Paro contained lucrative trade routes, it became the object of competition among aristocratic families.^{【116†[5]】}
L179: Although Bhutan generally enjoyed favorable relations with both Tibet and 【118†British India】 through the 19th century, extension of British power at Bhutan's borders as well as Tibetan incursions in British 【119†Sikkim】 defined politically opposed pro-Tibet and pro-Britain forces.^{【120†[6]】} This period of intense rivalry between and within western and central Bhutan, coupled with external forces from Tibet and especially the 【80†British Empire】, provided the conditions for the ascendancy of the Penlop of Trongsa.^{【116†[5]】}
L180: After the 【121†Duar War】 with Britain (1864–65) as well as substantial territorial losses (【122†Cooch Behar】 1835; 【123†Assam】 【124†Duars】 1841), armed conflict turned inward. In 1870, amid the continuing civil wars, the 10th Penlop of Trongsa, 【125†Jigme Namgyal】 ascended to the office of 48th 【96†Druk Desi】. In 1879, he appointed his 17-year-old son 【68†Ugyen Wangchuck】 as the 23rd Penlop of Paro.
L181: Jigme Namgyal reigned through his death 1881, punctuated by periods of retirement during which he retained effective control of the country.^{【126†[7]】}
L182: The pro-Britain Penlop 【68†Ugyen Wangchuck】 ultimately prevailed against the pro-Tibet and anti-Britain Penlop of Paro after a series of civil wars and rebellions between 1882 and 1885. After his father's death in 1881, Ugyen Wangchuck entered a feud over the post of 【81†Penlop of Trongsa】. In 1882, at the age of 20, he marched on 【73†Bumthang】 and 【72†Trongsa】, winning the post of Penlop of Trongsa in addition to Paro.
L183: In 1885, Ugyen Wangchuck intervened in a conflict between the 【99†Dzongpens】 of 【104†Punakha】 and 【105†Thimphu】, sacking both sides and seizing 【127†Simtokha Dzong】. From this time forward, the office of Desi became purely ceremonial.^{【126†[7]】}
L184: ### Nationhood under the Wangchucks
L185: 
L186: [【128†edit】]
L187: 
L188: King 【68†Ugyen Wangchuck】, receiving the order of the 【129†Knight Commander of the Order of the British Empire】 at 【130†Punakha Dzong】
L189: 
L190: Further information: 【68†Ugyen Wangchuck】, 【86†Jigme Wangchuck】, and 【87†Jigme Dorji Wangchuck】
L191: The 12th Trongsa Penlop, 【68†Ugyen Wangchuck】, firmly in power and advised by Kazi 【131†Ugyen Dorji】, accompanied the 【132†British expedition to Tibet】 as an invaluable intermediary, earning his first British knighthood. Penlop Ugyen Wangchuck further garnered knighthood in the 【133†KCIE】 in 1905. Meanwhile, the last officially recognized Shabdrung and Druk Desi had died in 1903 and 1904, respectively. As a result, a power vacuum formed within the already dysfunctional 【134†dual system of government】.
L192: Civil administration had fallen to the hands of Penlop Ugyen Wangchuck, and in November 1907 he was unanimously elected hereditary monarch by an assembly of the leading members of the clergy, officials, and aristocratic families. His ascendency to the throne ended the traditional 【134†dual system of government】 in place for nearly 300 years.^{【120†[6]】}^{【135†[8]】} The title 【81†Penlop of Trongsa】 – or Penlop of Chötse, another name for Trongsa – continued to be held by crown princes.^{【136†[9]】}
L193: As 【70†King of Bhutan】, Ugyen Wangchuck secured the 【137†Treaty of Punakha】 (1910), under which Britain guaranteed Bhutan's independence, granted Bhutanese Royal Government a stipend, and took control of 【138†Bhutanese foreign relations】. After his coronation, Uygen further merited the British 【139†Delhi Durbar】 Gold Medal in 1911; the 【140†Knight Commander of the Order of the Star of India】 (【141†KCSI】) in 1911; and the 【142†Knight Grand Commander of the Order of the Indian Empire】 (【133†GCIE】) in 1921.
L194: King Ugyen Wangchuck died in 1926.
L195: The reign of the Second King 【86†Jigme Wangchuck】 (1926–1952) was characterized by an increasingly powerful central government and the beginnings of infrastructure development. Bhutan also established its first diplomatic relations with 【82†India】 under the bilateral Treaty of Friendship, largely patterned after the prior Treaty of Punakha.^{【143†[10]】}
L196: The Third King 【87†Jigme Dorji Wangchuck】 (r. 1952–1972) ascended the throne at the age of 23, having been educated in 【144†England】 and 【82†India】. During the reign of the Third King, Bhutan began further political and legal reforms and started to open to the outside world.^{【126†[7]】} Notably, the Third King was responsible for establishing a unicameral 【145†National Assembly】 in 1953 and establishing relations with 【146†Indian Prime Minister】 【147†Jawaharlal Nehru】 in 1958.
L197: Under Jigme Dorji Wangchuck, Bhutan also modernized its 【148†legal codes】.^{【149†[11]】}
L198: ### Democratization under the Wangchucks
L199: 
L200: [【150†edit】]
L201: 
L202: Main articles: 【88†Jigme Singye Wangchuck】 and 【69†Jigme Khesar Namgyel Wangchuck】
L203: The Third King died in 1972, and the 【151†Raven Crown】 passed to the 16-year-old 【88†Jigme Singye Wangchuck】. The Fourth King was, like his father, educated in England and India, and had also attended Ugyen Wangchuck Academy at Satsham Choten in 【152†Paro】. Reigning until 2006, the Fourth King was responsible for the development of the tourism industry, 【153†Gross National Happiness】 as a concept, and strides in democratization including the draft 【154†Constitution of Bhutan】.
L204: The later years of his reign, however, also marked the departure of 【155†Bhutanese refugees】 in the 1990s amid the government's 【156†driglam namzha】 policy (official behaviour and dress code) and citizenship laws that were overzealously enforced by some 【157†district】 officials. To the surprise of the Bhutanese public, the Fourth King announced his abdication in 2005 and retired in 2006, handing the crown to his son 【69†Jigme Khesar Namgyel Wangchuck】.^{【126†[7]】}
L205: 【69†Jigme Khesar Namgyel Wangchuck】 assumed the throne as the Fifth King in 2008 as the kingdom adopted its 【154†first democratic Constitution】.
