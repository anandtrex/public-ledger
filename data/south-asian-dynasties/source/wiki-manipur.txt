L172: The Manipur Kingdom,^{【118†[1]】}^{【119†[2]】}^{【120†[3]】}^{【121†[4]】} also known as Meckley,^{【122†[5]】}^{【123†[6]】}^{【124†[7]】} was an ancient kingdom at the India–Burma frontier.^{【125†[8]】}^{【126†[9]】}^{【127†[10]】} Historically, Manipur was an independent kingdom ruled by the 【128†Meitei dynasty】.^{【129†[11]】}^{【130†[12]】}^{【131†[13]】} But it was also invaded and ruled over by 【132†Burmese kingdom】 at various points in time.^{【133†[14]】}^{【134†[15]】} It became a protectorate of the British 【135†East India Company】 from 1824, and a 【91†princely state】 of the 【92†British Raj】 in 1891.^{【136†[16]】} The princely state bordered the 【137†Assam Province】 in the west and 【138†British Burma】 in the east, and in the 20th century covered an area of 22,327 square kilometres (8,621 sq mi) and contained 467 villages.
L173: The capital of the state was 【84†Imphal】.
L174: ## Kangleipak State
L175: 
L176: The early history of Manipur is composed of mythical narratives. The location of the 【103†Kangla Fort】 on the banks of the 【139†Imphal River】 is believed to be where King Pakhangba built his first palace.^{【140†[17]】}
L177: 【141†Loyumba Shinyen†simple.wikipedia.org】, the written constitution of Kangleipak was formally developed by King 【142†Loiyumba】 (1074–1121) in 1110 AD.^{【143†[18]】} He consolidated the kingdom by incorporating most of the principalities in the surrounding hills.^{【144†[19]】} After subjugating all the villages within their valley, Kangleipak kings grew in power and began a policy of expansion beyond their territory.
L178: In 1443 King Ningthoukhomba raided 【145†Akla】 (present day 【146†Tamu, Myanmar】), an area ruled by 【147†Shan people】, initiating a policy of Manipuri claims to the neighbouring 【99†Kabaw Valley】.^{【144†[19]】} The zenith of the Kangleipak State was reached under the rule of King 【148†Khagemba】 (1597–1652). Khagemba's brother Prince Shalungba was not happy about Khagemba's rule so he fled to 【149†Taraf】 where he allied with the local 【150†Bengali Muslim】 leaders.
L179: With a contingent of Bengali Muslim soldiers led by 【151†Muhammad Sani】, Shalungba then attempted to invade 【71†Manipur】 but the soldiers were captured and made to work as labourers in Manipur. These soldiers married local 【152†Meitei women】 and adapted to the 【82†Meitei language】.
L180: They introduced 【153†hookah】 to Manipur and founded the 【154†Meitei Pangals】 (Manipuri Muslim community).^{【155†[20]】} It is claimed that Manipur learned the art of making gunpowder from the Chinese merchants who visited the state around 1630 and had started making rockets named Meikappi by the early 18th century.^{【156†[21]】}
L181: ## Manipur State
L182: In 1714, King 【157†Pamheiba】 was initiated into 【158†Gaudiya Vaishnavism】 by 【159†Shantidas Gosai】, a 【160†Bengali Hindu】 from 【161†Sylhet】. He made the 【162†Gaudiya Vaishnava】 faith the 【163†state religion】, replacing 【86†Meitei religion】, made the 【82†Meitei language】 (aka 【85†Manipuri language】) written in 【164†Bengali script】, destroying many documents of 【82†Meitei language】 written in 【165†Meitei script】 and changed his name to Garib Niwaj. In 1724, the 【166†Sanskrit】 name Manipur (transl.
L183: (translation) the City of Jewels) was adopted as the name of the state, to make the realm eponymous with 【167†Manipura of the Mahabharata】. King Garib Niwaj made several incursions into 【168†Burma】, but made no permanent conquest.
L184: After the death of Gharib Nawaz in 1751, Manipur was occupied by the 【169†Kingdom of Burma】 and the Meitei king 【116†Bhagyachandra】 (【82†Meitei】: 【116†Ching-Thang Khomba】) sought help from the British, but when the British refused help he went to Ahom King 【170†Rajeswar Singha】 who sent a force of 40,000 under Haranath Senapati Phukan to free Manipur.^{【171†[22]】} A treaty of alliance was negotiated in 1762 and a military force was sent to assist Manipur.
L185: The force was later recalled and then the state was left to its own devices.^{【172†[23]】} Manipur was invaded at the onset of the 【173†First Anglo-Burmese War】, together with 【174†Cachar】 and 【137†Assam】.
L186: ## British protectorate
L187: Following the 【175†Burmese occupation of Manipur】 and of 【176†Assam】, in 1824, the British declared war on Burma, which came to be known as the 【173†First Anglo-Burmese War】. The exiled Manipur prince 【111†Gambhir Singh】 sought British help for raising a force and the request was granted. 【177†Sepoys】 and artillery were sent and British officers trained a levy of Manipuri troops for the battles that ensued.
L188: With British help, 【111†Gambhir Singh】 succeeded in expulsion of Burmese from Manipur, after receiving additional reinforcement, he expelled the Burmese from 【99†Kabaw Valley】 by 1826 as well,^{【178†[24]】}^{【179†[25]】} and Gambhir Singh became the king of Manipur, after the war the Treaty of Yandabo was signed.^{【172†[23]】} According to the British 【180†political agent】 【181†McCulloch】, by the treaty of Yandabo, Manipur was declared independent but being too weak by itself to remain so, and its position being in a military point of view, of too much importance to permit the chance of the Burmese obtaining the command of it, the British government has been compelled to guard against such a chance and to retain in the country a political agent, all border disputes having been settled by this officer.^{【182†[26]】}^{【183†[27]】} However, the Burmese did not agree to the cession of Kabaw Valley.
L189: After prolonged negotiations, the British agreed to return Kabaw Valley to Burma, on the ground that Marjit Singh had already ceded it earlier. Some Manipuri scholars regard this as a lease to Burma.^{【184†[28]】} as the British paid a compensation of 500 Sicca Rupees per month.^{【185†[29]】}^{【186†[30]】} After these developments, Manipur is deemed to have become a 【187†British protectorate】,^{【188†[31]】} even though its ensuing status is debated till this day.^{【189†[32]】}
L190: At the death of Gambhir Singh, his son 【190†Chandrakirti Singh】 was only one year old, and his uncle 【191†Nara Singh】 was appointed as regent. That same year the British decided to restore the Kabaw Valley to the 【169†Kingdom of Burma】, which had never been happy about the loss.
L191: A compensation was paid to Raja of Manipur in the form of an annual allowance of 【192†Rs】 6,370 and a 【193†British residency】 was established in Imphal, the only town of the state, in 1835 to facilitate communication between the British and the rulers of Manipur.^{【172†[23]】}
L192: After a thwarted attempt on his life, Nara Singh took power and held the throne until his death in 1850. His brother 【194†Devendra Singh】 was given the title of Raja by the British, but he was unpopular. After only three months, the rightful heir Chandrakirti Singh invaded Manipur and rose to the throne. Numerous members of the royal family tried to overthrow Chandrakirti Singh, but none of the rebellions was successful. In 1879, when British Deputy Commissioner G.H.
L193: Damant was killed by an 【195†Angami Naga】 party, the king of Manipur assisted the British by sending troops to neighbouring 【109†Kohima】. Following this service to the crown, Chandrakirti Singh was rewarded with the 【196†Order of the Star of India】.
L194: After Maharaja Chandrakriti's death in 1886 his son 【197†Surachandra Singh】 succeeded him. As in previous occasions, several claimants to the throne tried to overthrow the new king. The first three attempts were defeated, but in 1890, following an attack on the palace by 【198†Tikendrajit】 and 【199†Kulachandra Singh】, two of the king's brothers, Surachandra Singh announced his intention to abdicate and left Manipur for Cachar.
L195: Kulachandra Singh then rose to the throne while Tikendrajit Singh, as the commander of the Manipuri armed forces, held the real power behind the scenes. Meanwhile, Surachandra Singh, after leaving Manipur, appealed to the British for help to recover the throne.^{【172†[23]】}
L196: ### The 'Manipur Expedition'
L197: 
L198: Main article: 【107†Anglo-Manipur War】
L199: 
L200: The British decided to recognise Kulachandra Singh as Raja, and to send a military expedition of 400 men to Manipur to punish Senapati Tikendrajit Singh as the main person responsible for the unrest and the dynastic disturbances. This action and the violent events that followed are known in British annals as the 'Manipur Expedition, 1891',^{【200†[33]】} while in Manipur they are known as the 'Anglo-Manipur War of 1891'.
L201: The British attempt to remove Tikendrajit from his position as military commander (Senapati) and arrest him on 24 March 1891 caused a great stir. The British Residency in Imphal was attacked and the Chief Commissioner for Assam 【201†J.W. Quinton】, Col. Sken, the British Resident and other British officials were murdered.
L202: In the middle of the unrest 【202†Ethel St Clair Grimwood】, the widow of Frank St Clair Grimmond, the killed British Resident, was credited with leading a retreat of surviving 【177†sepoys】 out of Manipur to Cachar.^{【203†[34]】} She was later lauded as a hero.^{【204†[35]】} A 5,000 strong punitive expedition was sent against Manipur on 27 April 1891.
L203: Three British columns entered Manipur from 【138†British Burma】, Cachar and the Naga Hills, which after several skirmishes with the 3,000 men strong Manipuri army, managed to pacify the kingdom. Following the British attack, Tikendrajit and Kulachandra Singh fled, but were captured.
L204: Tikendrajit and those Manipuris involved in the killing of the British officers were tried and hanged, while the deposed King Kulachandra Singh and other leaders of the rebellion were sent to the 【205†Cellular Jail】 in the 【206†Andaman Islands】. Manipur was briefly 【207†annexed】 to 【208†British India】 by virtue of the 【209†doctrine of lapse】.
L205: On 22 September 1891 when 【210†Meidingngu Churachand】 (Churachandra), a 5-year-old boy, was put on the throne, power was restored nominally to the Manipuri crown over the state. During the dynastic disturbances and the British intervention the Naga and 【211†Kuki】 hill tribes of the state lapsed into lawlessness, with numerous instances of murder and arson in the mountain villages, a situation that lasted well into 1894.^{【172†[23]】}
L206: ## Princely state under British Raj
L207: The child ruler Churachand belonged to a side branch of the Manipur royal family, so that all the main contenders to the throne were bypassed. While he was a minor the affairs of state were administered by the British 【180†Political Agent】, which facilitated the introduction and implementation of reforms.
L208: The first paved road to Manipur was inaugurated in 1900 —until then there had been no proper roads to reach the kingdom— and this improvement in communication facilitated a visit by Viceroy 【212†Lord Curzon】 in 1901. Raja Churachand was formally declared king in 1907 after completing education in 【213†Ajmer】.^{【172†[23]】} In 1918 he was given the privilege to use the title '【113†Maharaja】' and during his reign Manipur enjoyed a period of relative peace and prosperity.
L209: In 1934 king Churachand was 【214†knighted】 by the British, becoming Sir Churachandra Singh.^{【215†[36]】}
L210: Between March 1944 and July 1944 part of Manipur and the 【216†Naga Hills District】 of Assam Province were occupied by the 【217†Imperial Japanese Army】. The capital Imphal was shelled on 10 May 1944.^{【218†[37]】}
L211: ## Twilight and end of the princely state
L212: On 14 August 1947, with the lapse of paramountcy of the British Crown, Manipur regained its political autonomy that it had prior to 1891.^{【219†[38]】}^{【220†[a]】} The Maharaja had signed the 【221†Instrument of Accession】 on 11 August 1947, which legality is argued by many,^{【222†[41]】}^{【223†[42]】}^{【224†[43]】}^{【225†[44]】} ceding the three subjects of defence, external affairs and communications to the 【93†Union of India】, while retaining internal political autonomy.^{【226†[45]】}^{【227†[46]】}^{【228†[47]】}^{【229†[48]】} A '【230†Manipur State Constitution Act 1947】' was enacted, giving the state its own constitution, although this did not become known in other parts of India owing to the relative isolation of the kingdom.^{【231†[40]】} The Government of India did not recognize the Constitution.^{【232†[49]】}
L213: On 21 September 1949, the Maharaja was coerced to sign a Manipur Merger Agreement with the Union of India, to take effect on 15 October the same year, which legality is also disputed by many^{【233†[50]】}^{【234†[51]】}^{【235†[52]】} As a result of the agreement, the Manipur State merged into the Indian Union as a 【236†Part C State】 (similar to a 【237†Chief Commissioner's Province】 under the colonial regime or a 【238†Union Territory】 in the present Indian structure), to be governed by a Chief Commissioner appointed by the Government of India.
L214: The representative assembly of Manipur was abolished.^{【239†[53]】}
L215: Unhappy with the central rule, 【240†Rishang Keishing】 began a movement for representative government in Manipur in 1954.
L216: The Indian home minister, however, declared that the time was not yet ripe for the creation of representative assemblies in Part C States such as Manipur and 【241†Tripura】, stating that they were located in strategic border areas of India, that the people were politically backward and that the administration in those states was still weak.^{【239†[53]】} However, it was given a substantial measure of local self-government under the Territorial Councils Act of 1956, a legislative body and council of ministers in 1963, and 【71†full statehood】 in 1972.^{【242†[54]】}
