L253: The Indo-Greek Kingdom, also known as the Yavana Kingdom,^{【178†[a]】} were 【179†Hellenistic-era】 【180†Greek】 kingdoms in the northwestern part of the 【181†Indian subcontinent】 covering most of modern-day 【176†Pakistan】, parts of 【182†northwestern India】 and some eastern parts of 【175†Afghanistan】.^{【183†[3]】}^{【184†[4]】}^{【185†[5]】}^{【186†[6]】}^{【187†[7]】}^{【188†[8]】}
L254: The term "Indo-Greek Kingdom" loosely describes a number of various Hellenistic states, ruling from regional capitals like 【189†Taxila】, 【150†Sagala】, 【190†Pushkalavati】, and 【191†Bagram】.^{【192†[9]】}^{【193†[10]】}^{【194†[11]】} Other centers are only hinted at; e.g. 【195†Ptolemy】's 【196†Geographia】 and the nomenclature of later kings suggest that 【197†Theophilus】 in the south of the Indo-Greek sphere of influence may also have had a royal seat there at one time.
L255: The kingdom was founded when the 【198†Graeco-Bactrian】 king 【164†Demetrius I of Bactria】 invaded India from Bactria in about 200 BC.^{【199†[12]】} The Greeks to the east of the 【200†Seleucid Empire】 were eventually divided to the 【171†Graeco-Bactrian Kingdom】 and the Indo-Greek Kingdoms in the North Western Indian Subcontinent.^{【201†[13]】}
L256: During the two centuries of their rule, the Indo-Greek kings 【156†combined the Greek and Indian languages and symbols】, as seen on their coins, and blended Greek and Indian ideas, as seen in the archaeological remains.^{【202†[14]】} The diffusion of Indo-Greek culture had consequences which are still felt today, particularly through the influence of 【203†Greco-Buddhist art】.^{【204†[15]】} The ethnicity of the Indo-Greek may also have been hybrid to some degree.
L257: 【205†Euthydemus I】 was, according to Polybius,^{【206†[16]】} a 【207†Magnesian】 【208†Greek】. 【205†Euthydemus I】 was an 【209†Ionian-Greek】 from one of the Magnesias in 【210†Ionia】,^{【211†[17]】} though it is uncertain from which one (【212†Magnesia on the Maeander】 or 【213†Magnesia ad Sipylum】).^{【214†[18]】}^{【215†[19]】}^{【216†[20]】} His son, 【164†Demetrius I】, founder of the Indo-Greek kingdom, was therefore of Greek ethnicity at least by his father.
L258: A marriage treaty was arranged for the same Demetrius with a daughter of the 【200†Seleucid】 ruler 【217†Antiochus III】. The ethnicity of later Indo-Greek rulers is sometimes less clear.^{【218†[21]】} For example, 【219†Artemidoros】 (80 BC) was supposed to have been of 【220†Indo-Scythian】 descent, although he is now seen as a regular Indo-Greek king.^{【221†[22]】}
L259: 【166†Menander I】, being the most well known amongst the Indo-Greek kings, is often referred to simply as "Menander," despite the fact that there was indeed another Indo-Greek King known as Menander II. Menander I's capital was at Sakala in the Punjab (present-day Sialkot). Following the death of Menander, most of his empire splintered and Indo-Greek influence was considerably reduced.
L260: Many new kingdoms and republics east of the 【222†Ravi River】 began to mint new coinage depicting military victories.^{【223†[23]】} The most prominent entities to form were the 【224†Yaudheya】 Republic, 【225†Arjunayanas】, and the 【226†Audumbaras】. The Yaudheyas and Arjunayanas both are said to have won "victory by the sword".^{【227†[24]】} The 【228†Datta dynasty】 and 【229†Mitra dynasty】 soon followed in 【230†Mathura】.
L261: The Indo-Greeks ultimately disappeared as a political entity around 10 AD following the invasions of the 【220†Indo-Scythians】, although pockets of Greek populations probably remained for several centuries longer under the subsequent rule of the 【231†Indo-Parthians】, the 【232†Kushans】,^{【233†[b]】} and the 【173†Indo-Scythians】, whose 【234†Western Satraps】 state lingered on encompassing local 【235†Greeks】, up to 415 AD.
L262: ## Background
L263: ### Initial Greek presence in the Indian subcontinent
L264: 
L265: 【236†Pataliputra Palace capital】, showing Greek and Persian influence, early 【237†Mauryan Empire】 period, 3rd century BC.
L266: Greeks first began to establish authority in the Northwestern part of the Indian subcontinent during the time of the 【238†Persian】 【239†Achaemenid Empire】. 【240†Darius the Great】 conquered the area, but along with his successors also conquered much of the Greek world, which at the time included all of the western 【241†Anatolian peninsula】.
L267: When Greek villages rebelled under the Persian yoke, they were sometimes ethnically cleansed, their inhabitants relocated to the far side of the empire around today's Afghanistan. Over time they grew influential politically and rose to power establishing their own independent kingdoms and expanding their realm in all directions.
L268: In the fourth century BC, 【242†Alexander the Great】 defeated and conquered the Persian empire. In 326 BC, this 【243†included the northwestern part】 of the Indian subcontinent as far as the 【244†Hyphasis River】.
L269: Alexander established 【245†satrapies】 and founded several cities, including 【246†Bucephala】; he turned south when his troops refused to go further east.^{【247†[25]】} The Indian satrapies of the 【248†Punjab】 were left to the rule of 【249†Porus】 and 【250†Taxiles】, who were confirmed again at the 【251†Treaty of Triparadisus】 in 321 BC, and the remaining Greek troops in these satrapies were left under the command of Alexander's general 【252†Eudemus】.
L270: After 321 BC Eudemus toppled Taxiles, until he left India in 316 BC. To the south, another general also ruled over the Greek colonies of the Indus: 【253†Peithon, son of Agenor】,^{【254†[26]】} until his departure for 【255†Babylon】 in 316 BC.
L271: Around 322 BC, the Greeks (described as 【256†Yona】 or 【257†Yavana】 in Indian sources) may then have participated, together with other groups, in the uprising of 【258†Chandragupta Maurya】 against the 【259†Nanda dynasty】, and gone as far as 【260†Pataliputra】 for the capture of the city from the Nandas.
L272: The 【261†Mudrarakshasa】 of 【262†Visakhadutta】 as well as the 【263†Jaina】 work Parisishtaparvan talk of Chandragupta's alliance with the Himalayan king Parvatka, often identified with 【249†Porus】,^{【264†[27]】} and according to these accounts, this alliance gave Chandragupta a composite and powerful army made up of 【257†Yavanas】 (Greeks), 【265†Kambojas】, 【266†Shakas】 (Scythians), 【267†Kiratas】 (Nepalese), 【268†Parasikas】 (Persians) and 【269†Bahlikas】 (Bactrians) who took 【260†Pataliputra】.^{【270†[28]】}^{【271†[29]】}^{【272†[30]】}
L273: In 305 BC, 【273†Seleucus I】 led an army to the 【274†Indus】, where he encountered 【258†Chandragupta】. The confrontation ended with a peace treaty, and "an intermarriage agreement" (【275†Epigamia】, Greek: Ἐπιγαμία), meaning either a dynastic marriage or an agreement for intermarriage between Indians and Greeks.
L274: Accordingly, Seleucus ceded his eastern territories to Chandragupta, possibly as far as 【276†Arachosia】 and received 500 【277†war elephants】 (which played a key role in Seleucus's victory at the 【278†Battle of Ipsus】):^{【279†[31]】}
L275: > The Indians occupy in part some of the countries situated along the Indus, which formerly belonged to the Persians: Alexander deprived the Ariani of them, and established there settlements of his own. But 【280†Seleucus Nicator】 gave them to 【281†Sandrocottus】 in consequence of a marriage contract, and received in return five hundred elephants.
L276: 
L277: — 【282†Strabo】 15.2.1(9)^{【283†[32]】}
L278: The details of the marriage agreement are not known,^{【284†[33]】} but since the extensive sources available on Seleucus never mention an Indian princess, it is thought that the marital alliance went the other way, with Chandragupta himself or his son Bindusara marrying a Seleucid princess, in accordance with contemporary Greek practices to form dynastic alliances.
L279: An Indian 【285†Puranic】 source, the 【286†Pratisarga Parva】 of the 【287†Bhavishya Purana】, described the marriage of Chandragupta with a Greek ("【257†Yavana】") princess, daughter of Seleucus,^{【288†[34]】} before accurately detailing early Mauryan genealogy:
L280: > "【258†Chandragupta】 married with a daughter of 【289†Suluva】, the 【257†Yavana】 king of 【290†Pausasa】. Thus, he mixed the Buddhists and the Yavanas. He ruled for 60 years. From him, 【291†Vindusara】 was born and ruled for the same number of years as his father. His son was 【292†Ashoka】."
L281: 
L282: — Pratisarga Parva^{【293†[35]】}^{【288†[34]】}
L283: 
L284: 【294†Kandahar Bilingual Rock Inscription】 (【295†Greek】 and 【296†Aramaic】) by king 【292†Ashoka】, from 【297†Kandahar】, 【175†Afghanistan】.^{【298†[36]】}
L285: 【258†Chandragupta】, however, followed Jainism until the end of his life. He got in his court for marriage the daughter of 【280†Seleucus Nicator】, and thus, he mixed the Indians and the Greeks. His grandson 【292†Ashoka】, as Woodcock and other scholars have suggested, "may in fact have been half or at least a quarter Greek."^{【299†[37]】}
L286: Also several Greeks, such as the historian 【300†Megasthenes】,^{【301†[38]】} followed by 【302†Deimachus】 and 【303†Dionysius】, were sent to reside at the 【172†Mauryan】 court.^{【304†[39]】} Presents continued to be exchanged between the two rulers.^{【305†[40]】} The intensity of these contacts is testified by the existence of a dedicated Mauryan state department for Greek (【257†Yavana】) and Persian foreigners,^{【306†[41]】} or the remains of 【307†Hellenistic】 pottery that can be found throughout northern India.^{【308†[42]】}
L287: On these occasions, Greek populations apparently remained in the northwest of the Indian subcontinent under Mauryan rule. L303: Alexander had also established several colonies in neighbouring 【349†Bactria】, such as 【350†Alexandria on the Oxus】 (modern 【351†Ai-Khanoum】) and 【323†Alexandria of the Caucasus】 (medieval 【352†Kapisa】, modern 【191†Bagram】). After Alexander's death in 323 BC, Bactria came under the control of 【289†Seleucus I Nicator】, who founded the 【200†Seleucid Empire】.
L304: The Greco-Bactrian Kingdom was founded when Diodotus I, the 【245†satrap】 of Bactria (and probably the surrounding provinces) seceded from the Seleucid Empire around 250 BC. The preserved ancient sources (see below) are somewhat contradictory and the exact date of Bactrian independence has not been settled. Somewhat simplified, there is a high chronology (c. 255 BC) and a low chronology (c.
L305: 246 BC) for Diodotos' secession.^{【353†[59]】} The high chronology has the advantage of explaining why the Seleucid king 【354†Antiochus II】 issued very few coins in Bactria, as Diodotos would have become independent there early in Antiochus' reign.^{【355†[60]】} On the other hand, the low chronology, from the mid-240s BC, has the advantage of connecting the secession of Diodotus I with the 【356†Third Syrian War】, a catastrophic conflict for the Seleucid Empire.
L306: > Diodotus, the governor of the thousand cities of Bactria (【357†Latin】: Theodotus, mille urbium Bactrianarum praefectus), defected and proclaimed himself king; all the other people of the Orient followed his example and seceded from the Macedonians.
